01 · Place

A herd, a homeland, and Treaty 8

The Klinse-Za herd lives in the northern foothills of the Rocky Mountains, within the traditional territories of West Moberly First Nations and Saulteau First Nations.

Caribou are connected to food, teachings, cultural continuity, and responsibilities to future generations. Their decline therefore harmed more than a wildlife population.

04 · Knowledge and authority

Two knowledge systems can work together without becoming the same

Conservation science helps measure change. Nation-led knowledge and law help determine what recovery is for, and who has authority to decide.

Two-Eyed Seeing offers a useful course lens when applied carefully in this different cultural context. The goal is not to absorb Indigenous Knowledge into a Crown framework. Each system must retain integrity, and knowledge-sharing must be connected to decision-making power.

The meter lengths for authority and cultural relationship are conceptual, not measured data. They visualize the argument that these dimensions remain incomplete.

Therefore

Combining knowledge systems is not enough if final authority remains unequal. Durable recovery requires ecological repair and consent-based Indigenous co-governance.

06 · Future practice

Four moves from rescue to durable recovery

01

Fund Guardians for the long term

Support Nation-led monitoring, enforcement, knowledge transmission, and adaptive management beyond short grant cycles.

02

Repair habitat connections

Restore roads and linear disturbances, protect movement corridors, and prevent new fragmentation.

03

Make consent operational

Build land-use decisions around the Nations as governing authorities, not stakeholders consulted after priorities are set.

04

Measure what the Nations define

Report abundance and habitat alongside Nation-defined indicators of access, teaching, cultural use, and relationship renewal.

Recovery is complete only when

caribou are abundant,habitat is secure,and the Nations can renew their relationship with the herd.
