Indigenous-led recovery of the Klinse-Za caribou

The central question

Is recovery complete when population numbers rise, or only when habitat, Indigenous authority, and cultural relationships are restored?

01 · Place and relationship

A threatened herd in Treaty 8 territory

The Klinse-Za southern mountain caribou live in the northern foothills of the Rocky Mountains in northeastern British Columbia, within the traditional territories of West Moberly First Nations and Saulteau First Nations.

The landscape is more than habitat. The Twin Sisters mountains are culturally and spiritually significant to Treaty 8 First Nations, and caribou are tied to harvesting rights, food, teachings, and responsibilities to future generations. When the herd collapsed, the loss was ecological, cultural, and political.

02 · The conservation problem

The decline was not simply “natural”

Logging, roads, mining, and hydroelectric development fragmented mature forest. Disturbance also changed predator-prey relationships by creating access routes and early-seral habitat that favoured other prey, increasing predation pressure on caribou.

03 · Evidence of recovery

Population growth shows what coordinated action can achieve

West Moberly and Saulteau initiated an intensive recovery program that combined maternal penning, monitoring, predator reduction, and the pursuit of long-term habitat protection.

How to read this figure

The increase is substantial, but the bars do not prove that emergency interventions alone create a self-sustaining population. Maternal penning and wolf reduction can prevent immediate extirpation. They do not repair the industrial disturbances that reshaped the food web.

Course concept · Two-Eyed Seeing

Two-Eyed Seeing is a Mi'kmaw framework described by Iwama et al. (2009). Applied carefully in this different cultural context, its useful lesson is to draw on the strengths of distinct knowledge systems without absorbing one into the other. Here, coexistence also requires Indigenous decision-making authority, not only the collection of Indigenous Knowledge for a Crown-designed plan.

05 · Indigenous leadership

The Nations were not stakeholders in someone else’s project

West Moberly and Saulteau defined the crisis, initiated recovery, and built the governance needed to connect emergency population work with landscape protection.

Through the Nîkanêse Wah tzee Stewardship Society, the Nations led maternal penning and monitoring, advanced habitat restoration, and negotiated as governing parties in the 2020 partnership agreement.

06 · Critical insight

A comeback can still conceal unequal power

The case is successful because Indigenous leadership changed both population outcomes and the scale of habitat protection. Yet formal partnership does not automatically eliminate colonial authority.

01

Proximate cause versus structural cause

Wolves are a proximate source of mortality, but industrial habitat disturbance produced the altered food web. Continuing predator reduction without habitat repair shifts the consequences of development onto wolves and caribou.

02

Partnership versus implementation power

The Nations are governing parties, but Crown governments retain important regulatory and funding mechanisms. Funding gaps or industrial exceptions could weaken implementation even while population counts rise.

03

Survival versus meaningful recovery

A population target can measure biological persistence. It cannot by itself measure whether habitat is secure, Treaty rights can be exercised, or the relationship between Nations and caribou has been renewed.

Core argument

Combining knowledge systems is insufficient if one government still controls the final decision. Durable recovery requires knowledge coexistence and consent-based Indigenous co-governance.

07 · Future conservation practice

Move from emergency rescue to durable relationship

The next phase must make the recovery self-sustaining ecologically, culturally, and politically.

01

Fund Indigenous Guardians for the long term

Stable funding should support Nation-led monitoring, enforcement, knowledge transmission, and adaptive management beyond short grant cycles.

02

Repair habitat, not only population numbers

Restore roads and other linear disturbances, protect movement corridors, and prevent new industrial exceptions from fragmenting secure habitat.

03

Make consent part of development decisions

Treat West Moberly and Saulteau as governing authorities whose consent shapes land-use decisions, rather than as stakeholders consulted after priorities are set.

04

Report cultural and biological indicators together

Track abundance, recruitment, and habitat alongside Nation-defined measures such as access, teaching, harvesting readiness, and renewed relationships.

Recovery is complete only when

caribou are abundant,habitat is secure,and the Nations can safely renew their relationship with the herd.
